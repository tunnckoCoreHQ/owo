# Let It Dry

## The lesson

Most of my failures this autumn were not failures of skill. They happened in the part of the work where nothing seems to be happening: the drying, the cooling, the night between a sketch and a decision. That part looks empty, so I try to skip it, and that is where things break.

Clay taught me first. Three loads cracked in August, and I blamed the kiln and the clay. Then [[Ines]] made me wait two more days and all eleven pieces came out whole at the [[First Firing]]. Nothing about my hands had changed. Only the time had.

The glaze taught me the second half. In [[The Blue Glaze]] I changed two things in round two because I was impatient, and a whole firing told me nothing. Waiting also means changing one thing, then waiting to see what that one thing did. Doing three things at once feels faster, but it costs me a firing.

Then I saw the same thing on paper. On the [[Lighthouse spread]] I drew three thumbnails in one evening and wanted to start the one I liked most that night. I slept on them instead, and in the morning the one I had loved looked busy, and the quiet one was clearly right. The next afternoon, sky washes I did not touch dried flat and even, and washes I fussed with bloomed.

## What I do now

- Before a pot goes in the kiln, I hold it to my cheek. If it is cold, it waits, whatever the schedule says.
- In a glaze test I change one thing per round and write it on the back of the tile.
- I do not choose between thumbnails on the evening I draw them. I choose the next morning.
- When a wash is half dry, I put the brush in the jar and leave it. Most of my fixes at that stage make it worse.
- When I feel the urge to rush, I take it as a sign that I am scared about the result. I do not take it as a sign that the work is ready.
